Part 01

Why quantum?

Some problems grow exponentially. Nature itself computes them.

We start with motivation: what classical computers find hard, and why a machine built from quantum systems could do better on specific problems.

Why quantum

Where classical computers hit a wall

18 PB

Exponential state spaces

Storing the full state of just 50 quantum particles takes 2⁵⁰ complex numbers, about 18 petabytes. Molecules and materials are exactly such systems.

RSA

Hard mathematical problems

Factoring large integers and discrete logarithms have no known efficient classical algorithm. Internet security relies on that.

nm

Physical limits of scaling

Transistors now approach atomic dimensions, where quantum effects become a problem. Quantum computing turns them into the resource.

Part 02

The qubit

From a single two-level system to entangled registers, and why they are so fragile.

Part 2 builds the mathematical and physical picture of the qubit, which everything in the architecture section depends on.

The qubit

A qubit is a unit vector in two dimensions

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

Amplitudes

α and β are complex numbers. |0⟩ and |1⟩ are the computational basis states.

Normalisation

|α|² + |β|² = 1, because the two outcome probabilities must sum to one.

Two real angles

Dropping global phase: |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^(iφ) sin(θ/2)|1⟩.

10

Dirac (bra-ket) notation: |0⟩ = [1, 0]ᵀ, |1⟩ = [0, 1]ᵀ. A global phase e^(iγ) has no observable effect, so a single qubit state has two real degrees of freedom θ and φ — which leads directly to the Bloch sphere.

13

The joint state of n qubits lives in the tensor product space of dimension 2^n. This is why around 50 qubits is roughly the frontier of brute-force classical state-vector simulation, although tensor-network methods can go further for low-entanglement circuits.

The qubit

Entanglement: one state, many qubits

|Φ⁺⟩ = ( |00⟩ + |11⟩ ) / √2

Not separable

It cannot be written as (state of A) ⊗ (state of B). Neither qubit has a state of its own.

Perfect correlation

Measure one: 0 or 1 at random. The other always matches, yet no signal travels.

A resource

Powers teleportation, superdense coding, error correction and algorithmic speedups.

Quantum Computing · Qubits & Architecture

14

|Φ+⟩ is one of four Bell states. Entanglement correlations violate Bell inequalities — they cannot be explained by hidden classical variables — but they cannot transmit information faster than light because each individual outcome is random.

The qubit

The no-cloning theorem

No operation can copy an unknown quantum state.

Proved by Wootters, Zurek and Dieks in 1982. It follows directly from the linearity of quantum mechanics.

No backup copies

Classical redundancy (store it 3×, vote) does not apply.

Cleverer error correction

QEC spreads information through entanglement instead.

Secure communication

Eavesdroppers cannot copy qubits unnoticed, the basis of QKD.

Quantum Computing · Qubits & Architecture

15

Sketch of proof: if U|ψ⟩|0⟩ = |ψ⟩|ψ⟩ for all ψ, linearity applied to a superposition gives a contradiction. Teleportation moves a state but destroys the original, so it does not violate no-cloning.

Quantum Computing · Qubits & Architecture

18

Qubit count alone is a poor measure. A useful rule of thumb: the number of gates you can run reliably is roughly 1 / error rate. At 99.9% fidelity that is about a thousand operations — far short of what serious algorithms need, which is why error correction matters.

Quality over quantity

A logical qubit is built from many noisy physical qubits. Useful machines need hundreds to thousands of logical ones.

Physical qubits: the hardware you fabricate. Logical qubits: the error-corrected qubits your algorithm uses.

This distinction is the thread through the architecture section. Today's "100+ qubit" chips count physical qubits. IBM's Starling target, for example, is about 200 logical qubits running 100 million gates.

Gates & circuits

It is not “trying every answer at once”

Amplitudes can be negative or complex. When computational paths meet, they add (constructive) or cancel (destructive).

A quantum algorithm is designed so wrong answers cancel and the right answer is amplified before measurement.

Example: H then H

|0⟩
→ H → (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2
→ H → ½(|0⟩+|1⟩) + ½(|0⟩−|1⟩)
= |0⟩

The two |1⟩ paths have opposite signs and cancel exactly.

Shor’s algorithm and the crypto clock

How it works

  1. Reduce factoring N to finding the period of aˣ mod N
  2. Quantum Fourier transform reveals the period
  3. Classical number theory turns the period into factors

What it threatens

RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve crypto. A 2025 estimate (Gidney) puts RSA-2048 at under 1 million noisy qubits in under a week.

The response

NIST post-quantum standards (2024): FIPS 203 ML-KEM, 204 ML-DSA, 205 SLH-DSA. Migrate now: “harvest now, decrypt later”.

Resource estimates for breaking RSA-2048 have fallen from about 20 million qubits (2019) to under a million (2025) through better algorithms and error-correction tricks — not bigger hardware. That trend is why organisations are migrating to post-quantum cryptography now.

Outlook

Three eras, and what each unlocks

NOW

NISQ & utility

100–1,000+ noisy qubits, error mitigation, hybrid HPC workflows.

Unlocks: physics simulation, first advantage claims (IBM target: 2026).

~2026–2030

Early fault tolerance

Tens to hundreds of logical qubits. IBM Starling (2029): 200 logical, 100M gates.

Unlocks: chemistry and materials beyond classical reach.

2030s

Scaled fault tolerance

Thousands of logical qubits. IBM Blue Jay (2033+): 2,000 logical, 1B gates.

Unlocks: Shor-scale cryptanalysis, broad optimisation.

Key takeaways

What to remember

01

Qubits use superposition, entanglement and interference; measurement returns only bits.

02

Speedups come from structured problems, not from “trying everything”.

03

A quantum computer is a full stack: mostly classical engineering around a fragile core.

04

Error correction turns many physical qubits into few logical ones; it is the path to scale.

05

Several platforms compete; fault-tolerant machines are targeted for the late 2020s.
