Agent Harness Architecture: A Survey

2026-10-06 · Prof. Engr. Faiz ul Haque Zeya

Abstract

The performance and reliability of an LLM agent depend on its harness: the software that runs the model's loop, exposes tools, manages context and state, and enforces and checks what the agent does. During 2026 the harness became an object of study in its own right, with formal definitions, taxonomies, source-code studies of production systems and automated harness optimisers. These works use incompatible decompositions. This survey reviews the literature from an architectural standpoint. It reconciles the published decompositions into a reference architecture of eight responsibilities in three planes (control, resource and assurance) plus an extension surface, and gives a crosswalk between them. For each responsibility it reviews the design space and the evidence from source-level studies. It classifies eight architectural styles, compares representative systems, and examines the harness as an optimisation target, the evaluation of harnesses separately from models, and formal assurance. Three findings stand out. The control and resource planes are converging across independently built systems. The assurance plane is defined inconsistently and implemented least. Automated evolution, harness-aware evaluation and formal guarantees all depend on that plane. The survey closes with ten open problems and a set of candidate safety and liveness properties for harness verification.

Keywords: agent harness, harness engineering, LLM agents, software architecture, reference architecture, context management, agent evaluation, formal verification

1 Introduction

An LLM agent is a language model plus the software that runs it. That software, the agent harness, decides what the model sees on each turn, which actions exist, when the loop stops and what is checked before a result is accepted. Until 2025 it was mostly treated as glue code around the model.

In 2026 the harness became a named object of study. Practitioner essays defined it (Trivedy, 2026; Lopopolo, 2026; Rajasekaran, 2026). Surveys formalised it (Meng et al., 2026; Ning et al., 2026). Source-code studies dissected production systems (Rombaut, 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Barbaste et al., 2026). Optimisers began to rewrite harnesses automatically (Lee et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026).

The reason is evidence that the harness changes outcomes at fixed model weights. SWE-agent showed early that the design of the agent-computer interface affects issue resolution for the same model (Yang et al., 2024). Zhang et al. (2026a) argue that for long-horizon tasks among comparable frontier models, harness configuration often explains more performance variance than model choice. A benchmark score is therefore a joint measurement of a model and a harness.

The literature has grown faster than its vocabulary. Published decompositions disagree on the parts of a harness: six components, seven layers, three layers with twelve dimensions, seven subsystems. Most surveys catalogue components or projects. The questions a software architect asks are scattered across papers: which decisions are structural, which quality attributes they trade against each other, and which styles recur.

This survey takes the architectural view. It makes six contributions.

  1. A reference architecture of three planes and one extension surface that reconciles the published decompositions, with a crosswalk between them (Sections 4 and 5).

  2. A review of the design space of each architectural concern, grounded in source-level studies of production harnesses (Sections 6 to 12).

  3. A classification of architectural styles and a comparison of representative systems (Section 13).

  4. An account of the harness as an optimisation target, and of what automated evolution requires from the architecture (Section 14).

  5. A review of how harnesses are evaluated and formally assured (Sections 15 and 16).

  6. A research agenda of open problems (Section 17).

The field is young and most of its sources are 2026 preprints. Section 3 states what was covered and how far the evidence can be trusted.

2 Background and definitions

2.1 Agent = Model + Harness

The working definition in the field is subtractive: the harness is everything in an agent that is not the model. Trivedy (2026) states it as Agent = Model + Harness. Seong et al. (2026) adopt it and define the harness as every piece of code, configuration and execution logic outside the model.

Other definitions are constructive and name the parts. Barbaste et al. (2026) define the harness as the runtime that couples an LLM to the world through a loop, tools, context management, safety controls, orchestration and extension surfaces. Zhang et al. (2026a) call it the infrastructure layer that governs context construction, tool interaction, orchestration and verification. Lee et al. (2026) use a narrower reading: the code that determines what information to store, retrieve and present to the model.

The definitions agree on one point. The harness, not the model, fixes what the agent can perceive, which actions it can take, and how its work is orchestrated and verified (Seong et al., 2026).

2.2 Formal definitions

Five formalisations are in use. They differ in what they treat as the primary object: a set of components, a configuration, a controller, or a trajectory generator.

SourceFormal objectParts or properties
Meng et al. (2026)Tuple H = (E, T, C, S, L, V)Execution loop, tool registry, context manager, state store, lifecycle hooks, evaluation interface
Guo et al. (2026)Tuple H = (I_obs, C, L, I_act, S, V)Observation interface, context manager, control loop, action interface, state and artifact store, verification and governance
Chen et al. (2026)Pair H = (M, C)Model configuration, and a harness configuration made of typed processors attached to lifecycle hooks
Zhang et al. (2026a)Controller C_H over a stochastic policyClosed-loop control with three properties: stability, context drift, control lag
Zhang et al. (2026b)Trajectory of steps (s, a, o, u)State, model action, observation, and the harness update to state, memory or plan

Meng et al. (2026) also give the execution loop a labeled-transition-system semantics. Its states run from idle through model invocation, tool dispatch and state commit to termination. Safety and liveness properties are stated over that system, which makes the loop a candidate for model checking (Section 16).

The component tuples describe structure. The controller and trajectory views describe behaviour, and they explain why small structural changes have large effects: the harness closes the loop around an open-loop policy (Zhang et al., 2026a).

2.3 Terminology

Four terms overlap in the literature.

  • Scaffold is the older word for the code around the model in coding agents. Rombaut (2026) uses it for the control loop, tool definitions, state management and context strategy.

  • Harness is the 2026 term and usually implies the full production runtime, including safety, persistence and extension.

  • Framework means a library for building harnesses. Meng et al. (2026) separate full-stack harnesses, development frameworks and capability modules by their position in the stack.

  • Evaluation harness means infrastructure that runs agents against benchmarks. Meng et al. (2026) trace the execution harness partly to this software-testing lineage.

The distinction between framework and harness has empirical weight. Barbaste et al. (2026) report that none of eleven production coding harnesses imports a general-purpose agent framework. All eleven run on hand-written asynchronous loops.

2.4 A moving boundary

The line between model and harness is not fixed. Functions move across it in both directions. Context compaction is a harness function in most systems, yet AutoCompact trains the decision into the model's policy (Zhang et al., 2026d). Guo et al. (2026) describe this as a fourth engineering paradigm, agent-native training with co-evolution, after prompt, context and harness engineering.

A reference architecture therefore describes responsibilities, not permanent owners. Section 5 is written in those terms.

3 Scope and method

This is a narrative review, not a systematic one, and it makes no claim of completeness. Sources were found by keyword search on 6 October 2026 and by following the reference lists of the surveys found. Search terms covered agent harness, harness engineering, scaffold architecture, context compaction, harness evaluation and formal verification of agent orchestration.

A work was included if it treats the harness as its object of study. Five kinds qualified:

  • surveys and taxonomies of the harness;

  • source-level studies of harness architecture;

  • systems that optimise or generate harnesses;

  • evaluation methodology that separates harness from model;

  • formal specification or verification of harness behaviour.

Component-level work, for example on context compression or skills, was included only where it bears on an architectural decision. Work on model training, prompting technique and benchmark construction in general was excluded.

The reference list has 33 entries. Thirteen were consulted in full text, sixteen at abstract level, and four are cited as characterised by other sources in the list. Each entry is tagged accordingly. Four limits apply to everything that follows.

  1. Maturity of sources. Most sources are 2026 preprints that have not been peer reviewed. The peer-reviewed exceptions are noted in the reference list.

  2. Domain skew. Coding agents dominate the evidence because their source code is public. Claims may not transfer to web, embodied or scientific agents.

  3. Unreproduced numbers. Every figure is quoted as its source reports it. None was reproduced for this survey.

  4. Version drift. Preprints change between versions. Counts and results cited here are tied to the versions listed in the references.

Three observations follow from the table.

First, the decompositions overlap but do not coincide. All of them contain a loop, tools and context. They differ on whether state, safety, observability, orchestration and extension are separate parts or aspects of other parts.

Second, the two bodies of work rest on different evidence. The surveys reason from literature and public documentation. The source studies reason from code. This review found no published mapping between their vocabularies.

Third, architectural decisions and their consequences are covered unevenly. Xia et al. (2026a) state forces and consequences, but only for skills. Barbaste et al. (2026) catalogue patterns for coding harnesses. Guo et al. (2026) map task pressures to harness configurations. Among the works reviewed, no single account covers the whole harness at that level.

This survey is a synthesis at that level. It contributes no new corpus. It aligns the existing decompositions, reads the source-level evidence through one structure, and adds the literature on optimisation, evaluation and formal assurance that the earlier surveys treat briefly or as future work.

5 A reference architecture

5.1 Three planes and an extension surface

The published decompositions reduce to eight responsibilities in three planes, plus one surface through which the harness is changed. The grouping follows the question each plane answers.

Control plane: what happens next.

  • Execution loop. Sequences turns, calls the model, dispatches tools, and decides when to stop, retry or recover.

  • Orchestration. Splits work across sub-agents and roles, hands off between them, and routes subtasks to models.

Resource plane: what the model can see and do.

  • Tool interface. Holds the registry and schemas of actions, shapes observations, and provides the environment in which actions run.

  • Context management. Assembles each prompt, retrieves what is relevant, and compacts history when the window fills.

  • State and persistence. Keeps transcripts, memory, checkpoints and artefacts across turns and sessions.

Assurance plane: whether it is allowed, correct and visible.

  • Governance. Enforces permissions, approvals, isolation and budgets, usually through interception points around actions.

  • Verification. Checks work against tests, verifier models or contracts before it is accepted.

  • Observability. Records traces, costs and outcomes as evidence for people, evaluators and optimisers.

Extension surface: how the harness is changed. Configuration files, skills, plugins, protocol servers and hooks let people and automated optimisers alter harness behaviour without editing its core.

The accent marks the assurance plane, which three independent studies find the least implemented (Section 11.4). People and optimisers close an outer loop: they read evidence from that plane and change the harness through the extension surface.

Three readings follow.

The control and resource planes are settled. Every decomposition has a loop, tools and context. The sources differ only in granularity, for example whether state is separate from context.

The assurance plane is not. Guo et al. (2026) merge verification and governance. Li et al. (2026a) split observability, verification and governance into three layers. The source-code studies name safety as a subsystem but have no verification subsystem. In code, verification appears as a loop primitive such as generate-test-repair (Rombaut, 2026) or as a pattern such as verify-on-stop guards (Barbaste et al., 2026).

The extension surface is named only by those who read code. Barbaste et al. (2026) list extensibility as a subsystem and Liu et al. (2026) describe four extension mechanisms in Claude Code. The surface matters beyond convenience. It is the interface on which automated harness optimisers act (Section 14).

5.3 Quality attributes and tensions

Each plane carries different quality attributes, and the documented tensions fall between planes.

PlanePrimary quality attributesDocumented tension
ControlTermination, recoverability, cost, latencyDeeper loops spend more without necessarily producing more useful feedback (Zhang et al., 2026b)
ResourceCapability, context efficiency, continuityBroader tool menus and persistent memory enlarge operational risk (Li et al., 2026a)
AssuranceSafety, correctness, auditabilityStronger controls raise token, latency and infrastructure cost (Li et al., 2026a)
ExtensionModifiability, portabilityChanges that help one layer or one model can degrade the whole or fail to transfer (Li et al., 2026a; Wu et al., 2026)

Meng et al. (2026) argue that coupling between components, more than failure of any single one, drives unreliability. Their examples are conflicts between context management and lifecycle hooks, and between state mutation and tools. The sections that follow take one responsibility at a time, then return to coupling in Section 17.

6 Control plane: loop and orchestration

6.1 Loop primitives

The execution loop is built from a small set of primitives that systems combine. Rombaut (2026) identifies five in thirteen open-source coding agents.

PrimitiveWhat it does
ReActAlternates model reasoning, one action and one observation
Generate-test-repairProduces a candidate, runs a check, and repairs on failure
Plan-executeSeparates a planning phase from an execution phase
Multi-attempt retryRestarts the task from a clean state after a failed attempt
Tree searchExplores alternative trajectories, up to full Monte Carlo tree search

Seven of the thirteen agents use ReAct as their primary structure, and eleven compose more than one primitive. Rombaut concludes that scaffolds are better described as compositions along continuous spectra than as discrete types.

Barbaste et al. (2026) reach a compatible result for eleven production harnesses. They find three loop shapes: a plain iterative action-observation loop, a reflection-augmented loop with lint and test feedback, and a coordinator-worker arrangement. Nine of the eleven systems run the plain iterative loop.

6.2 The core loop is small

In production harnesses the loop itself is simple, and most of the code surrounds it. Liu et al. (2026) describe the core of Claude Code as a while-loop that calls the model, runs tools and repeats. The minimal case in Barbaste et al. (2026) is a linear while-loop. The maximal case is an event-sourced conversation with parallel action batches.

Two findings qualify the value of loop complexity. Barbaste et al. (2026) observe that loop sophistication does not predict benchmark performance, and that the bulk of production code serves safety, user experience and extensibility. Dai (2026) finds three harnesses with opposed design philosophies converging on one standardised loop.

6.3 Who drives the loop

Rombaut (2026) classifies the loop driver as the user, the LLM or the scaffold. The choice correlates with retrieval strategy. Scaffold-driven agents invest in preprocessing, such as repository graphs and hierarchical narrowing. LLM-driven agents defer retrieval to general shell tools.

A related split is between sampling and iteration. Some agents run many independent attempts and vote. Most LLM-driven agents refine one trajectory in depth. Rombaut reads this as unresolved uncertainty about whether breadth or targeted feedback is worth more.

6.4 Orchestration

Orchestration extends the loop across several model contexts. Five of Rombaut's thirteen agents support sub-agent spawning, through five different mechanisms:

  • spawn, send and resume exposed to the model as tools;

  • specialised agent roles with enforced permissions;

  • delegation through the same event stream as other actions;

  • mode switching between planning and acting;

  • nesting of subgraphs in a workflow graph.

Production systems show the same spread. Barbaste et al. (2026) report recursive composition with forked context in Claude Code, threads with dedicated mailbox channels in Codex, and a planner, workers and a verifier over a shared blackboard in Hermes. Aider has no orchestration by design.

Two architectural rules recur. First, sub-agents isolate context: in Claude Code a sub-agent returns only summary text to its parent, which prevents context growth in the parent (Liu et al., 2026). Second, roles separate production from judgement. Rajasekaran (2026) describes planner, generator and evaluator roles for long-running work, and Seong et al. (2026) keep the evaluator adversarial and separate from the agent that edits.

6.5 Stopping, recovery and budget

Termination is usually handled by simple mechanisms. The minimal mechanism is a cost limit and a step limit (Barbaste et al., 2026). Loop detection is handled differently across systems. One harness routes it through the permission system, so the user decides whether a repeating agent continues.

The evidence favours stopping rules based on the value of feedback over fixed budgets. Zhang et al. (2026b) compare seven harness families and find that raw spend predicts success poorly. A companion layer that ranks feedback actions by expected gain, and stops or rolls back low-value loops, raised mean pass rate from 61.2% to 68.2% and cut mean cost from 213.8 to 85.1 units under matched settings.

The same study finds that harness efficiency depends on the task. Deep closed-loop harnesses were most efficient on small executable tasks. Simpler harnesses were strongest on the repository-level slice. Loop design is therefore a harness-task interaction, not a property of the harness alone.

7 Tool and environment interface

7.1 The agent-computer interface

The tool interface defines the action space and the form of every observation. SWE-agent established that its design matters at fixed model capability (Yang et al., 2024). Guo et al. (2026) split it into an observation interface and an action interface, which treats shaping what comes back as a separate decision from defining what can be called.

7.2 How many tools, and which

Tool counts vary by two orders of magnitude while functional coverage barely varies. Rombaut (2026) finds counts from 0 to 37 across thirteen agents, yet the same four capability categories in every LLM-driven agent: read, search, edit and execute. Barbaste et al. (2026) find a range from one tool, a shell, to more than a hundred in a multi-channel assistant.

Granularity is therefore a design choice independent of capability. Three findings guide it.

  • Dedicated edit tools win. All eleven production harnesses in Barbaste et al. (2026) privilege file-edit tools over raw shell editing.

  • Fewer visible tools can be better. Meng et al. (2026) report that removing tools often improves performance more than adding them, and treat the registry as the primary point of behavioural control.

  • Ambiguity costs efficiency. In the controlled study of Zhang et al. (2026b), higher tool-selection ambiguity and noisier observations both reduce the share of spend that turns into useful feedback. Better routing gave the largest gain of any factor tested.

7.3 Discovery and deferred loading

Large registries create a context problem, since every tool description consumes prompt space. Deferred loading addresses it. Claude Code ships 43 typed tools, marks some as deferred, and lets the model find them through a search tool when needed (Barbaste et al., 2026). Another system exposes 69 tools behind scoped toolsets.

The same staging appears for skills. Xia et al. (2026a) describe progressive activation, in which a short descriptor is visible for selection and the full body loads only on activation. Dai (2026) lists gradual context expansion among five patterns on which independent harnesses converged.

7.4 Retrieval as a tool

Code retrieval in production harnesses is deterministic. None of the eleven systems studied by Barbaste et al. (2026) retrieves code with vector embeddings. All use text search, syntax-tree parsing, file globbing and auto-discovered context files.

7.5 The execution environment

Actions need somewhere to run, and the choice of substrate is shared with governance. Li et al. (2026a) make execution a layer of its own, covering managed sandboxes, micro-virtual machines and browser environments. Rombaut (2026) records operating-system sandboxing, container isolation and editor-integrated checkpoints among the options in use.

Ning et al. (2026) take the broader view that code is the medium of the whole interface. In their account, tests, simulators and execution logs give the agent an inspectable model of its environment.

7.6 Protocols

Tool-level standardisation is further along than agent-level standardisation. Meng et al. (2026) judge tool protocols mature and inter-agent protocols fragmented. Adoption counts in Section 12 support this. Liu et al. (2026) add a cost perspective: protocol servers carry the highest context cost of the four extension mechanisms in Claude Code.

8 Context management

8.1 Strategies in deployed systems

Context management is where deployed harnesses differ most. Rombaut (2026) finds seven compaction strategies in thirteen agents and names compaction, state management and model routing as the dimensions with the widest divergence.

StrategyMechanism
No managementHistory grows until the window overflows
Rule-based truncationOlder content is cut by fixed rules
Structural isolationContext is reset per retry or capped per phase
Token-based selective inclusionContent is admitted against a token budget
LLM summarisationA model condenses older history
Summarisation with verificationThe summary is validated by a separate check
LLM-initiated compactionThe model calls a tool to condense its own context

Rombaut separates prevention from cure. Prevention bounds growth structurally. Cure lets history grow and compresses it when the window fills. LLM summarisation, a cure, is the most common strategy in the sample.

8.2 Layered pipelines

Mature harnesses stack several strategies and run the cheap ones first. Liu et al. (2026) document five layers in Claude Code.

  1. Budget reduction caps the size of each tool result.

  2. Snip trims older history segments.

  3. Microcompact compresses at fine grain with awareness of the prompt cache.

  4. Context collapse projects a reduced view over the history at read time.

  5. Auto-compact writes a model-generated summary as a last resort.

The ordering reflects cost. Barbaste et al. (2026) report related techniques elsewhere: graph-based distillation, cache-tiered prompt assembly, and compaction that preserves lineage. They also describe a pattern in which the harness maintains separate prompt-cache strategies per model provider.

8.3 Where the decision lives

The compaction decision can sit in three places, and each has a published design.

PlacementExampleReported result
In the harness, by ruleLength-triggered summarisation, the common deployed practiceBaseline practice
In the model's policyAutoCompact (Zhang et al., 2026d) trains when to compact, what to keep and how to continue+9.2 and +5.0 points pass rate on two coding benchmarks over the base model
In an external managerAdaCoM (Yi et al., 2026) trains a separate model to edit the context of a frozen agentGains on web search and deep research benchmarks

AdaCoM reports a fidelity-reliability trade-off. Stronger agents benefit from keeping more raw context. Weaker agents need more aggressive compression to stay in a regime where their reasoning is reliable. A context manager is therefore fitted to an agent, and it transferred best between agents of similar capability.

Two further designs treat context as something else. ContextEvo evolves a context policy from long-horizon trajectories and reports results comparable to or better than three prominent harnesses (Li et al., 2026b). Ledger replaces transcript reading with an explicit execution state of what was observed, modified and attempted (Wang et al., 2026). It raised resolution from 56.2% to 64.2% on 500 coding tasks with one model at 28.9% lower cost.

8.4 Context as a lifecycle

Yan et al. (2026) reframe compression as lifecycle management with six intervention points: admission, placement, compaction, recovery, reuse and governance. Their classification adds two axes that matter to architects. One is reversibility, lossy against recoverable. The other is implementation, external scaffold against learned policy.

The governance stage is the weak one. Yan et al. describe it as largely normative in production agents. They note that compression can blur privilege boundaries and can silently disable safety instructions, and that no surveyed production system tracks the provenance of compressed content. Section 10 returns to this.

9 State, persistence and recovery

9.1 From destructive to event-sourced

State handling runs along one axis: how much history survives. Rombaut (2026) places thirteen agents on it. At one end a harness overwrites history with its summary. At the other, every event is immutable and compaction only adds markers over the log. Between them sit two special cases. One workflow-graph agent keeps a separate message list per model node. Tree-search agents keep statistics and file snapshots per search node.

Production harnesses have moved toward the append-only end. Liu et al. (2026) describe Claude Code sessions as mostly append-only files that record messages, tool results and compaction events. Dai (2026) lists append-only session records among the five patterns on which three independent harnesses converged.

9.2 What an append-only log buys

An append-only log makes four capabilities cheap. Barbaste et al. (2026) document the first two as patterns.

  • Resume and fork. A session can be continued or branched from any point, giving version control over the session tree.

  • Resumable loops. If the log also serves as the work queue, a crashed loop restarts from the last entry.

  • Audit. The record of what the agent saw and did is complete by construction.

  • Replay for optimisation. Past trajectories remain available as evidence for harness evolution (Section 14).

9.3 Memory across sessions

Persistence beyond one session is less settled. Barbaste et al. (2026) find a range from session-only memory to cross-session pipelines that the agent maintains itself. Liu et al. (2026) list cross-session persistence among six open design directions. Meng et al. (2026) describe a three-way trade-off in memory design among recency bias, scalability and retrieval latency.

9.4 Artefacts as state

Long-running harnesses move state out of the transcript and into files. Rajasekaran (2026) describes structured handoff artefacts between planner, generator and evaluator. Pan et al. (2026) make durable artefacts and state conventions explicit parts of a harness specification. In their ablation, file-backed state improved a computer-use benchmark by 5.5 points, mainly through auditability.

Ledger takes the idea furthest (Wang et al., 2026). It derives an explicit execution state from the history and checks proposed actions against it, returning earlier results that are still valid.

9.5 State and trust

State persistence interacts with governance. In Claude Code, resuming or forking a session does not restore session-scoped permissions, so each session is its own trust domain (Liu et al., 2026). Xia et al. (2026a) argue that stable identifiers for artefacts, versions, runs and decisions are a precondition for any later verification. Without them, evidence from separate mechanisms can only be loosely correlated.

10 Governance

10.1 A range of mechanisms

Governance mechanisms span from two counters to a three-layer stack. Barbaste et al. (2026) give the minimum as cost and step limits. They give the maximum as policy rules, an LLM reviewer of proposed actions, and an operating-system sandbox on three platforms.

Rombaut (2026) lists the mechanisms found in thirteen agents:

  • human supervision of every change;

  • per-tool approval settings;

  • configurable policy engines with per-tool rules;

  • a separate model that evaluates the safety of tool calls;

  • operating-system sandboxing;

  • container isolation;

  • editor-integrated checkpoints.

Only one of the thirteen used a model to judge another model's tool calls. Rombaut names safety, with compaction and state, as an area without ecosystem consensus.

10.2 Layered, deny-first designs

The most detailed public account is of Claude Code (Liu et al., 2026). Its permission system has seven modes, from plan-only through classifier-based automatic approval to a bypass mode. Evaluation is deny-first with escalation to the user.

The authors count seven independent safety layers around an action: tool pre-filtering, deny-first rules, mode constraints, automatic classification, shell sandboxing, non-restoration of permissions on resume, and hook-based interception. They derive the design from stated principles, among them defence in depth, externalised programmable policy and reversibility-weighted risk.

The same study shows that governance style follows deployment context. A repository-bound command-line tool emphasises per-action safety. A persistent multi-channel assistant emphasises perimeter-level access control.

10.3 Policy moves out of the prompt

Governance is migrating from prompt text to configuration and code. Barbaste et al. (2026) compare the same harnesses one quarter apart and find behavioural policy moving from prompt prose to feature flags. They report that none of the eleven system prompts contains policy-level language for refusing harmful requests. Enforcement sits elsewhere.

Two mechanisms carry it. Lifecycle hooks intercept actions before and after execution, and Meng et al. (2026) make them one of six harness components. Structured permission rules replace free text. One harness parses shell commands with a syntax tree before deciding whether to allow them.

Li, Wu and Chang (2026) explain why this layer stays outside the model. Capability-bearing functions can migrate into model weights as they become trainable. Constraint-bearing functions such as permissions and auditing keep their value only if they are separable from the thing they constrain.

10.4 Three boundaries that leak

Three documented weaknesses occur where governance meets another responsibility.

Delegation. Cai et al. (2026) model sub-agent spawning and identify four flaws: insecure memory inheritance, weak resource control, stale post-spawn state and improper termination authority. A compromised parent can pass instructions and corrupted state to its children.

Compaction. Yan et al. (2026) note that compression can blur privilege boundaries and silently drop safety instructions. By implication, a constraint stated early in a session is not guaranteed to survive repeated summarisation unless the harness re-injects it.

Extension. A skill may name a tool without being entitled to use it. Xia et al. (2026a) call the needed separation skill-execution authority separation: a reference to a capability must never act as a grant of authority.

Meng et al. (2026) add the threat model. They list prompt injection through the environment, escalation through tool composition and poisoning of persistent memory, and state that no production defence against cross-agent prompt injection exists.

10.5 Where governance is built

Open-source coverage of governance is thin. In the ecosystem map of Li et al. (2026a), execution, tooling, lifecycle and verification have the densest open-source coverage. Observability and governance are more prevalent in commercial platforms. Research on harness governance therefore works from a partial view.

11 Verification and observability

11.1 Verification inside the loop

Verification in deployed harnesses is a set of patterns, not a subsystem. The source-level studies record five forms.

FormWhere it sitsSource
Generate-test-repairA loop primitive that runs a check after each candidateRombaut (2026)
Dedicated validation toolsA fifth tool category beside read, search, edit and executeRombaut (2026)
Verified summarisationA check on a compaction summaryRombaut (2026)
Verify-on-stop guardsReflection moved to the loop exitBarbaste et al. (2026)
Verifier roleA separate agent or sub-agent type that judges outputLiu et al. (2026); Barbaste et al. (2026)

The verifier role is described in most detail in practitioner accounts. Rajasekaran (2026) describes an evaluator with explicit grading criteria that was calibrated over several rounds. Seong et al. (2026) specify an adversarial evaluator with four functions: checking claimed observations against real environment state, checking each success criterion, auditing time spent, and scoring.

11.2 Why verification pays

Controlled evidence ties outcomes to the quality of feedback, and verification is one of its main sources. Zhang et al. (2026b) credit a feedback event only if it is informative, valid, non-redundant and retained. In matched-budget synthetic experiments, improving feedback quality raised success from 0.27 to 0.90 with no change in cost or tool calls. Verifier strength was the second-largest contributor to harness efficiency after routing.

Guo et al. (2026) relate the form of verification to the task. Tasks with a strong oracle, such as tests, suit verifier loops and repair cycles. Tasks with a weak or delayed oracle need provenance tracking, review and approval.

Structure alone is not enough. Pan et al. (2026) find that explicit modules help when they align intermediate signals with the benchmark's acceptance criteria, and not otherwise.

11.3 Observability

Observability is the record that makes the other two assurance functions possible. Li et al. (2026a) define it as capturing traces, costs, failures and reliability signals. Meng et al. (2026) define an evaluation interface that instruments execution for offline analysis.

Three designs raise it from logging to an architectural element.

  • Matched observability pillars. Lin et al. (2026) give every editable component a file-level representation, distil raw trajectories into a layered evidence corpus, and pair every harness edit with a prediction that the next round checks.

  • Run-scoped bills of materials. Xia et al. (2026a) propose a per-run record linking skill artefacts, versions and participation decisions to run evidence.

  • Trajectory metrics. Zhang et al. (2026a) propose recovery rate after an anomaly, context retention per step, and control lag between detection and correction.

11.4 The least built plane

Three independent studies find assurance functions the least implemented part of the harness.

  • Meng et al. (2026) report that 14 of 22 systems under-implement the evaluation interface, often by conflating it with lifecycle hooks. The number of systems counted differs between versions of that survey.

  • Xia et al. (2026a) map eight systems to nine responsibility blocks. The three evidence and feedback blocks receive three direct realisations in 24 cells. Independent verification of skill use receives none.

  • Dai (2026) finds external verifiability absent from all three harnesses studied: no independent party can validate what the runtime did without trusting it.

Liu et al. (2026) name the consequence the observability-evaluation gap, between what a system can measure and what it can meaningfully evaluate. It heads their list of open design directions.

12 Extension surfaces

12.1 Four mechanisms

A modern harness is designed to be changed from outside its core. Liu et al. (2026) identify four extension mechanisms in Claude Code and rank them by the context they consume.

MechanismWhat it addsContext cost
Protocol servers (MCP)External tools over several transportsHigh
PluginsPackaged bundles of several componentsMedium
SkillsDomain instructions loaded on demandLow
HooksCode run at lifecycle eventsNone by default

The ranking shows that extension is not free. Each mechanism trades capability against context consumed, which ties the extension surface to context management.

12.2 Adoption

The same mechanisms recur across vendors. Barbaste et al. (2026) count adoption in eleven production harnesses at their July 2026 releases.

MechanismSystems adopting, of 11
Skill files9
MCP tool protocol8
ACP agent protocol6

Skill files lead the tool protocol in adoption. The agent protocol has taken on a second role, hosting one harness inside another. At the extreme, one harness implements every capability as an extension.

12.3 Skills as a sub-architecture

Skills have their own architectural literature. Xia et al. (2026a) review 37 systems and 51 papers and derive ten patterns in four layers: supply chain, mediation, execution control, and evidence and feedback. Their central distinction is between a skill artefact at rest and a skill in use during a run.

This survey reads that distinction as applying to every extension. An extension is static text or code in a repository. Its effect depends on admission, activation, authority and interpretation by a stochastic model at run time. Xia et al. argue that accountability therefore shifts from the content of the artefact to its participation in a specific run.

12.4 From tool to platform

Barbaste et al. (2026) argue that in the first half of 2026 the coding harness turned from a tool into a platform. Their evidence is fourfold: harnesses became importable libraries, marketplaces and migration tooling appeared, enterprise governance layers emerged, and whole agents became addressable behind a model-style endpoint.

Convergence accompanied the turn. The longitudinal comparison shows one vendor adopting another's hook vocabulary verbatim and a third reading a competitor's plugin format. The authors describe convergence becoming imitation. Dai (2026) independently finds defined integration points among five convergent patterns.

A further step is the meta-harness in the orchestration sense: a layer that coordinates several vendor harnesses behind one interface. This is distinct from Meta-Harness, the optimiser discussed in Section 14.

12.5 Harnesses as data

Three research systems push extension to its limit by making the whole harness a manipulable object.

  • Natural-language harnesses. Pan et al. (2026) express harness control as an editable document with contracts, roles, stages, adapters, state semantics and a failure taxonomy. A shared runtime interprets it. Task outcomes were comparable to code harnesses on coding and computer-use benchmarks.

  • Typed composition. Chen et al. (2026) assemble harnesses from typed processors attached to lifecycle hooks and edit them through a substitution algebra.

  • Plugin libraries. Shi et al. (2026) represent a harness as a composition of atomised plugins that are improved independently and recombined.

Each makes harness structure explicit enough to be compared, ablated and searched. That is the property automated optimisation needs.

13.3 Convergence

Four sources report convergence among independently built harnesses.

  • Rombaut (2026): the same four tool capability categories in every LLM-driven agent.

  • Meng et al. (2026): systems mature enough for production implement all six components.

  • Dai (2026): three harnesses from opposed philosophies share five patterns.

  • Barbaste et al. (2026): vendors copy each other's hook vocabularies and plugin formats within one quarter.

Convergence has a cost for research. If harnesses share structure, a comparison between products measures tuning more than architecture. This suggests that the informative architectural contrasts now lie between styles, for example a deployed full-stack harness against a declarative one.

14 The harness as an optimisation target

14.1 Systems

Since March 2026 at least seven systems have edited harnesses automatically while holding model weights fixed. They differ in what is edited and in how an edit is accepted.

14.2 What evolution asks of the architecture

Four requirements recur across the systems. Each is an architectural property of the harness being optimised.

  1. Explicit, revertible structure. Edits need a well-defined action space. File-level components, typed processors, plugins and natural-language harness documents all provide one.

  2. Rich evidence. Lee et al. (2026) attribute their gains to giving the proposer uncompressed access to prior code and traces. Lin et al. (2026) distil trajectories of millions of tokens into a corpus the optimiser can read.

  3. Attribution. An edit must be linked to its effect. Decision observability does this per edit. Zhang et al. (2026b) do it per feedback event, and use the score to gate evolution updates.

  4. A regression gate. Self-Harness accepts an edit only after regression testing on held-in and held-out tasks. Xia et al. (2026a) make the same demand for skills: run evidence may propose a change, but a shared artefact changes only after validation.

These map onto the reference architecture. Requirement 1 is the extension surface. Requirements 2 and 3 are observability. Requirement 4 is verification. A harness with a weak assurance plane is therefore hard to evolve safely, whatever the optimiser.

14.3 Does an evolved harness transfer?

The evidence on transfer across models conflicts.

  • For transfer. Lee et al. (2026) report one discovered harness improving five held-out models. Lin et al. (2026) report gains of 5.1 to 10.1 points across three other model families without re-evolution. Shi et al. (2026) report that evolved harnesses keep their advantage on other solver models.

  • Against transfer. Zhang et al. (2026c) start from the premise that effective harness design is model-specific. Wu et al. (2026) find that evolution gains are unstable, transfer only partly to held-out tasks, and depend strongly on the model executing the harness.

Wu et al. (2026) also report the limits of models as harness builders. Harnesses built by six models from a minimal seed underperformed human-designed ones on coding and research tasks. They matched or exceeded reference implementations on writing and machine-learning tasks.

The disagreement may reflect what was evolved. General engineering knowledge, such as verification steps, plausibly transfers. Compensation for one model's weaknesses plausibly does not. No study reviewed here separates the two.

14.4 Co-evolution

Three sources extend evolution to the model. Chen et al. (2026) report a ceiling beyond which a fixed model limits further harness gains, and feed trajectories back as training signal. Guo et al. (2026) name joint improvement of model and harness as the fourth engineering paradigm. Li, Wu and Chang (2026) predict the outcome: capability moves into the model, and constraint stays in the harness.

14.5 An unexamined risk

Acceptance in these systems is by task score or regression test. In the descriptions reviewed for this survey, none states a check that governance properties, such as approval gates or sandbox boundaries, survive an edit. Ning et al. (2026) list harness improvement without regression as an open challenge. Section 17 treats invariant-preserving evolution as a research problem.

15 Evaluating harnesses

15.1 The confound

A benchmark score for an agent is a joint measurement of model and harness, and most leaderboards report it as a property of the model. Zhang et al. (2026a) call the claim that the harness dominates the Binding Constraint Thesis. For long-horizon tasks among comparable frontier models, they argue, variance from harness configuration often equals or exceeds variance from model choice.

They collect published cases in which the harness changed and the model did not.

BenchmarkWhat changedReported effect
SWE-bench ProSame model moved from the SEAL scaffold to Claude Code45.9% to 55.4%
Terminal-Bench 2.0Prompt, middleware and verification changed around a fixed model52.8% to 66.5%
SWE-bench VerifiedSame model moved from SWE-agent to a vendor scaffold58.6% to between 72% and 75%

Their own factorial experiment crosses three models with three harnesses on 100 tasks. It reports a ratio of harness-induced to model-induced variance of 7.80 and six ranking reversals among nine pairings. The experiment is small, and the thesis is explicitly scoped to long-horizon tasks and comparable models.

15.2 Disclosure and protocols

The proposed remedy has three parts (Zhang et al., 2026a).

  • A harness card. A structured disclosure in seven layers: execution, tool, context, scheduling, observability, verification and governance.

  • Two valid protocols. A locked-harness protocol applies one specified harness to all models. A factorial protocol varies both and reports harness-induced variance per model, model-induced variance per harness, their ratio, and the number of ranking reversals.

  • Trajectory metrics. Recovery rate, context retention and control lag supplement final success.

Standardised evaluation harnesses address the same problem from the infrastructure side. The Holistic Agent Leaderboard is a standardised evaluation harness that cuts evaluation time from weeks to hours, and it reports 21,730 agent rollouts across nine models and nine benchmarks (Kapoor et al., 2026). Meng et al. (2026) cite it as evidence that part of what earlier work counted as agent failure was harness failure.

15.3 Measuring harness efficiency

Raw spend is a poor measure of what a harness achieves. Zhang et al. (2026b) propose Effective Feedback Compute, which counts only feedback that is informative, valid, non-redundant and retained. On real execution traces, tokens, tool calls, wall time and cost all fitted failure rates worse than a constant. The proposed coordinate, normalised by task demand, reached an R-squared of 0.93.

The measure separates two quantities that a single score hides. Harness efficiency is effective feedback per unit of spend. Task demand sets how much feedback a task needs. In the study, efficiency varied by benchmark for the same harness, which argues against ranking harnesses on one number.

15.4 Evaluating harness construction

HarnessDev moves the unit of evaluation from the task output to the harness itself (Wu et al., 2026). A creator model builds a harness from a minimal seed, then evolves it with feedback. A separate executor model runs the frozen harness on held-out tasks. The benchmark scores capability and execution-token cost over six creator models, four domains and 2,207 task instances.

Separating creator from executor is the methodological contribution. It distinguishes the quality of a harness from the fit between a harness and the model that wrote it.

15.5 What a full evaluation needs

The surveys add three requirements.

  • Several objectives at once. Guo et al. (2026) argue that task success hides differences in efficiency, latency, safety and process quality.

  • Diagnosis before credit. Li, Wu and Chang (2026) separate diagnosing where a model falls short from measuring whether harness support compensates, and at what net benefit.

  • Controlled ablation. Pan et al. (2026) show that a shared runtime allows harness modules to be removed one at a time, which ordinary code harnesses make difficult.

No protocol reviewed here covers all of these.

16 Formal assurance

16.1 What can be proved

The harness is deterministic software around a stochastic component. No proof can say what the model will output. A proof can say what the harness does for every output the model might produce. On this reading, which is this survey's, the model is treated as a nondeterministic environment and harness properties are stated over all of its behaviours.

Meng et al. (2026) supply the starting formalism. They model the execution loop as a labeled transition system. Its states are idle, observing, invoking the model, dispatching a tool, awaiting a tool result, committing state and terminated. Its alphabet covers model responses, tool invocations, tool results, human approvals and errors. They state safety and liveness properties over reachability of termination, and isolate environmental nondeterminism at tool-call boundaries.

16.2 Verified coordination

The most developed application of model checking found in this review is to multi-agent coordination. TraceFix synthesises a protocol topology from a task description, generates coordination logic in PlusCal, and repairs it using counterexamples from the TLA+ model checker until verification succeeds (Xia et al., 2026b). Verified process bodies become per-agent prompts. A runtime monitor rejects coordination operations outside the verified topology.

The reported results are concrete.

  • All 48 tasks reached full verification. 62.5% passed at the first attempt and none needed more than four repair iterations.

  • Verification finished in under 60 seconds per task, across state spaces spanning six orders of magnitude.

  • Under a fixed runtime, verified protocols cut deadlock and livelock from 31.1% to 14.1%.

  • Runtimes using the verified protocol degraded at about half the rate of prompt-only baselines when a weaker model was substituted.

The last result is the most significant for architecture. A verified structure absorbs part of the loss from a weaker model, which is the harness doing measurable work.

16.3 Contracts and types

Lighter formal devices appear in several systems.

  • Harness contracts. Natural-language harnesses bind each stage to required inputs and outputs, validation gates, permission boundaries, and retry and stop rules (Pan et al., 2026).

  • Skill contracts. A verifiable skill contract attaches independently checkable criteria to a skill, and a separate verifier returns passed, failed, inconclusive or verifier error (Xia et al., 2026a). The authors note that this pattern is more research-informed than mature in practice.

  • Typed composition. Typed processors restrict which harness edits are well formed (Chen et al., 2026).

  • Falsifiable edits. Pairing each harness edit with a prediction turns evolution into a sequence of testable claims (Lin et al., 2026).

16.4 Candidate properties

The mechanisms surveyed in Sections 6 to 12 suggest a set of properties a harness should satisfy for every model behaviour. The list below is this survey's proposal. None of the sources states it in this form.

PropertyKindResponsibility constrained

16.5 The gap

Formal methods have so far been applied to artefacts written for the purpose: coordination protocols and harness specifications. This review found no study that extracts a model from the source of a production harness and checks it. Meng et al. (2026) list formal verification of natural-language harness specifications, end-to-end behavioural guarantees and formal security models among their research directions, which suggests the gap is recognised.

A related absence is noted by Dai (2026). None of three harnesses studied lets an independent party verify what the runtime did without trusting the runtime.

17 Open problems

Ten problems follow from the review. Each is stated with the evidence that makes it open.

  1. Architecture of the assurance plane. Published decompositions disagree on whether verification, observability and governance are one part or three (Section 5.2), and three studies find these functions least implemented (Section 11.4). A reference design for the plane, with interfaces to the other two, does not exist.

  2. Verification of production harnesses. Formal methods have reached coordination protocols and purpose-written specifications (Section 16). Extracting a checkable model from a real harness, and proving properties such as those in Section 16.4, is untried in the work reviewed.

  3. Invariant-preserving evolution. Harness optimisers accept edits on task score or regression tests (Section 14.5). A method that guarantees governance properties across automated edits would let evolution run with less supervision.

  4. Governance through compaction and delegation. Compression can drop safety instructions, and sub-agents can inherit more than they should (Section 10.4). Both need mechanisms with stated guarantees and benchmarks that measure constraint retention over long sessions.

  5. Transfer of harness knowledge. Studies disagree on whether an evolved harness transfers across models (Section 14.3). Separating general engineering knowledge from compensation for one model's weaknesses would resolve it.

  6. Fault attribution. A failed run may be a model failure or a harness failure, and Meng et al. (2026) suggest earlier work confused the two. A method to assign failures to a plane of the architecture would make both evaluation and evolution more precise.

  7. Component coupling. Meng et al. (2026) and Li et al. (2026a) both report that local improvements can degrade the whole. Models of interaction between responsibilities, for instance between context management and hooks, are missing.

  8. Harness-aware evaluation at scale. Factorial designs are expensive, and the one reported uses 100 tasks (Section 15.1). Cheaper protocols, standard disclosure and efficiency measures validated beyond coding are needed.

  9. Beyond coding agents. Almost all source-level evidence is from coding harnesses. Guo et al. (2026) argue that the bottleneck moves by domain: verification in software, grounding in web interaction, safety in medicine, control in embodied agents. The reference architecture should be tested against those.

  10. Where the boundary settles. Functions are moving into the model (Section 2.4). If capability moves inward and constraint stays outside, as Li, Wu and Chang (2026) predict, the long-run harness is mostly an assurance plane. That prediction is testable as models change.

Problems 1 to 4 concern assurance. They are also the ones on which software engineering methods, more than machine learning methods, have most to offer.

18 Conclusion

The agent harness now has definitions, taxonomies, source-level anatomies and optimisers, almost all produced during 2026. This survey read them as one architecture. Eight responsibilities in three planes, with one extension surface, account for the decompositions published so far.

The control and resource planes are converging. Independent harnesses share a simple loop, the same tool categories, append-only state and layered context management. Their differences are increasingly matters of tuning.

The assurance plane is where architecture is unsettled. Its parts are defined inconsistently, implemented least, and more prevalent in commercial platforms than in open source. It is also the plane on which automated evolution, harness-aware evaluation and formal guarantees all depend.

If capability continues to move into models, the durable part of the harness will be the part that constrains, checks and records. That is where the open problems of Section 17 concentrate.
